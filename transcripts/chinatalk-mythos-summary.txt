Welcome to this special episode. Today we're diving deep into a fascinating conversation from ChinaTalk, one of the leading newsletters covering China's technology landscape. The discussion was hosted by Jordan Schneider and featured two of the most sharp observers of Chinese AI: Matt Sheehan from the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, and Kevin Xu, author of the widely read Interconnected newsletter. The topic is what they call China's Mythos moment — the inevitable point, perhaps only months away, when a Chinese AI lab produces a model powerful enough to hack through major cyber infrastructure. The question at the heart of the conversation: how will Beijing respond when that moment arrives, and what does it mean for the global AI landscape?

Let's set the stage. Claude Mythos, an enormously powerful AI model released in the United States, completely scrambled American AI policy. The Trump administration, which had built its identity on a let-it-rip regulatory approach and explicit promises not to overregulate AI, was caught entirely off guard. As analyst Dean Ball memorably put it, America suddenly found itself with a de facto involuntary licensing pre-approval regime for frontier models — imposed not by legislation or regulatory design, but by the sheer reality of what these models can do. The administration that promised the opposite of regulation was now scrambling to control access to frontier AI.

This raises the central question: will Beijing also freak out? A Zhipu AI co-founder publicly called his shot, telling Elon Musk on X that China will have a model on par with Mythos before the end of 2026. Even if he's a few months over-optimistic, the American think tank IAPS puts the date at February 2027. The bottom line is that a Chinese Mythos-level model is coming, and it's coming soon. Politics is now shaping not just the hardware ecosystem through export controls and chip restrictions, but the model layer itself.

Jordan Schneider opened the discussion by asking Matt Sheehan whether China's current relatively hands-off approach to AI is sustainable. The observation was that so far, Chinese AI regulation hasn't fundamentally changed how these businesses operate. We've seen ripples, regulators tipping their hand about concerns, but nothing approaching the crackdowns on Jack Ma's empire, Didi's ill-fated IPO, or the devastating edtech restrictions. The first real global impact on a model provider came not from Beijing but from Trump taking Claude Fable offline. Is the live-and-let-live approach stable?

Matt Sheehan's response was layered and worth unpacking carefully. He first pushed back on the premise that Beijing has been completely chill. There was a genuine period of regulatory impact back in 2023, the year after ChatGPT launched. During that period, Chinese AI companies were holding up model releases for three to six months while regulators constructed a generative AI regulation framework from scratch. The initial approvals functioned essentially as licenses — you needed explicit permission to release. Those have since evolved into something more like a registration system, but it remains a burdensome compliance regime. Companies must run and submit extensive batteries of tests. It represents a significant investment of employee hours, and crucially, the overwhelming majority of that testing effort goes toward content: political content, social issues, and ensuring that AI systems properly integrate with China's broad internet censorship and information management apparatus.

But here's where it gets interesting. The new question is what happens when a fundamentally different category of threat emerges — not content-related, but AI-enabled cyberattacks. Matt's argument is that China has built the infrastructure to handle this about as smoothly as a country possibly can. He drew a sharp contrast with the American experience. During the Mythos crisis from April through July, Washington's response was characterised by stumbling and fumbling. The recurring, embarrassing question was: who's in charge here? Does this go to the Center for AI Standards and Innovation? Through Commerce? Through the NSA? Different agencies were stepping on each other's toes, with no clear chain of command.

In China, the bureaucratic channel is clear. Before any company releases a model, it must submit test results to the Cyberspace Administration of China — the CAC. Companies don't have to hand over model weights, but they must provide API access and testing accounts. The CAC reviews, tests, and either approves or requests changes. Matt's prediction is that China's first response to a Mythos-level model will be to bolt cybersecurity-related tests onto this existing pre-deployment regime. We're already seeing early signs of this approach: the CAC recently ran a rectification campaign on AI applications, and one concrete outcome was that MiniMax, a major Chinese AI company, was forced to add safeguards against generating malicious code. If the capability jump is dramatic enough, Matt expects China to run what amounts to a government version of Project Glasswing — restricting initial access to government agencies, then state-owned enterprises, then major companies, before eventually allowing wider release. The policy documents that have leaked so far, notably from influential thinkers rather than official government sources, consistently identify the first priority as hardening government systems and critical infrastructure.

Kevin Xu broadly agreed with Matt's framework but added several important layers of context. He pointed out that China has far more prior art in AI regulation than most people realise. The deep synthesis rules — which govern AI-generated content — date back to 2021 and 2022, before ChatGPT even existed. China already has mandatory requirements to label AI-generated content across all social media platforms. Users on Douyin, the Chinese version of TikTok, or on Xiaohongshu, know whether what they're seeing was created by AI. This kind of transparency is something the United States hasn't even begun to implement at scale.

Kevin also pushed back on the framing of cybersecurity as an unmitigated disaster. His key insight is that while a Mythos-level model can discover and exploit vulnerabilities, it is equally capable of discovering and patching those same vulnerabilities on the defensive side. Even before Mythos-level capabilities, AI coding tools were already proficient at finding bugs. What the new generation of models adds is long-horizon task execution — the ability to autonomously carry out complex, multi-step operations over hours or even weeks. That's what you actually need to exploit a serious cybersecurity vulnerability. It's not about looking at one codebase and finding a flaw; it's about cross-referencing multiple databases, identifying patterns across disparate systems, and chaining together a sequence of exploits. Mythos-level models can apparently do this — but the same capability can be used defensively to identify and patch those exact vulnerability chains before adversaries exploit them. Kevin's base case for China's response is a measured, delayed release where critical infrastructure gets patched first, but the governance framework remains essentially intact.

This led to one of the conversation's most interesting debates: would China replicate something like Project Glasswing? Jordan outlined two scenarios. In the first — the Glasswing scenario — a leading Chinese AI lab like Zhipu or Alibaba informs the CAC that they've developed a model capable of massive cyber disruption. Access is then rolled out in stages: government first, then state-owned enterprises, then select private companies, with full public release coming months later after systems have been hardened. The second, more dramatic scenario involves a company simply releasing a dramatically more capable model without going through extended pre-release consultations.

Matt made a compelling case that the second scenario is highly unlikely in China's political environment. The CAC maintains remarkably close touchpoints with AI companies — far more intimate than anything in the American system. He shared a striking anecdote: the CAC regularly calls in government relations and policy teams from AI companies for meetings. At one point, around the time of the Hollywood writers' strikes, the CAC summoned company representatives specifically to discuss the labour implications of AI — not because they were planning immediate regulation, but because they wanted to understand emerging issues. In this environment, where regulators and companies are in constant dialogue, it would be extraordinarily bold and potentially self-destructive for a Chinese tech company to simply drop a frontier model without giving the CAC advance notice. Kevin reinforced this point: no Chinese company has ever skipped the heads-up or registration process, even with today's far less capable models.

Jordan pushed back on their status-quo assumptions by pointing to the American experience. There was a status-quo base case in the United States too, and it was shattered. The American government's relationship with these models transformed virtually overnight when the reality of Mythos-level capabilities became clear. Treasury Secretary Bessent was personally calling bank CEOs. This wasn't about content moderation anymore — it was about the potential for hospital systems to go offline and financial institutions to be compromised. Jordan argued that once the Chinese government truly internalises the national security implications, the response could be far more dramatic than simply adding a few cybersecurity questions to the existing registration form.

This is where Kevin offered perhaps the most important structural insight of the entire conversation. He pointed out that Project Glasswing in the United States was initiated by a private company — Anthropic — not by any government agency. Anthropic chose which organizations received early access. The US government didn't start the process; it was forced to respond to decisions made by a private corporation. That's why the American government has been scrambling ever since — their entire posture had been to avoid overregulation, and suddenly a private company had created a de facto licensing regime that the government never asked for.

In China, Kevin argued, the dynamics would be fundamentally reversed. If a similar situation arose, it would almost certainly be initiated by a government agency, with the access list pre-approved before any rollout began. The company wouldn't be driving the process — the state would. This is a crucial difference that means the Chinese response, while potentially similar in structure to Glasswing, would be more orderly from a governmental perspective because the government would be in the driver's seat from day one.

So who would make the guest list for a Chinese Glasswing? Kevin's prediction was straightforward: all government agencies, State Grid, the major petroleum companies, and strategic resource entities. He made an interesting observation that parallels the American situation — the entities with the worst, most vulnerable legacy IT systems would paradoxically get priority access for patching. If your infrastructure is running outdated software full of vulnerabilities, you need the defensive capabilities of a Mythos-level model most urgently.

Matt added a fascinating dimension to this analysis. The identity of the company that first develops the model matters enormously. If it emerges from Zhipu, what happens to Alibaba? Enormous amounts of government and commercial infrastructure run on Alibaba Cloud, and leaving it insecure would be unthinkable. The government might step in and essentially mandate cooperation between competing companies. More dramatically, Matt raised an extreme scenario: what if the security organs decide this is too important to leave in private hands? What if they say, essentially, thank you for bringing us to this point, but national security requires that we take over? Matt considers this unlikely — security organs don't know how to run AI companies or train frontier models, and nationalising the industry would likely destroy the innovation pipeline. But he noted that government confidence has been rising sharply since DeepSeek's breakthrough, which demonstrated that Chinese labs can compete at the frontier. When the government feels confident and doesn't feel it desperately needs the companies, it tends to crack down harder. That was the pattern of the tech crackdown era. The post-ChatGPT period, when Beijing felt insecure about falling behind, saw companies given more freedom. The DeepSeek moment may have shifted that balance back toward government assertiveness.

The conversation then turned to what may be the most consequential question for the global AI ecosystem: the future of open source in China. The timing was particularly relevant because in the days before this conversation, the founders of both MiniMax and Zhipu had made high-profile public commitments to open source. The MiniMax CEO committed one percent of the company's market cap to establishing a foundation dedicated to supporting open source in perpetuity. Zhipu's founder published an internal memo — strategically leaked onto Xiaohongshu — committing to open source as part of a mission to bring AI to all of humanity.

These statements came against the backdrop of a significant Reuters report revealing that Chinese authorities had been holding meetings with top tech firms about potentially restricting overseas access to China's most advanced AI models, including those not yet released. Participants reportedly discussed limits on both closed and open models, and officials floated the idea of making the leak or theft of proprietary AI an offense under national security law. The scope was apparently still under discussion, but the very fact that these conversations were happening represented a new signal.

Kevin noted the fascinating timing: on essentially the same day the Reuters report appeared, a Chinese official was at a United Nations AI gathering in Geneva publicly committing to open source as a cornerstone of China's global AI posture. Mixed signals to say the least — leaked reports suggesting restriction on one hand, and official diplomatic commitments to openness on the other. Kevin suggested that the clearest resolution would come at the Shanghai World AI Conference, where Xi Jinping himself was scheduled to deliver the keynote address.

Matt offered an important analytical clarification about the Reuters report. The regulators mentioned were from the Ministry of Commerce and the National Development and Reform Commission — the NDRC — not the CAC. Commerce is not the primary AI regulator; their involvement suggests this is about export controls rather than domestic regulation. China has done this before: ahead of the TikTok saga, the Ministry of Commerce wrote recommendation algorithms into China's controlled items list, giving Beijing a veto over any forced sale. This could be a similar move — creating leverage and legal authority rather than signaling an imminent ban.

Matt also emphasised the diplomatic narrative that China has carefully constructed around open source. Their 2023 Global AI Governance Initiative, released just before the Bletchley AI Safety Summit, explicitly positioned China as the champion of global AI access, standing against what they characterised as American technological hegemony. The framing is simple but powerful: the US wants to control AI and restrict access; China is the country of openness and sharing. This narrative has been enormously valuable to Beijing on the global stage, particularly in courting the Global South. Even if they eventually decide to slow-walk open source releases for security reasons, Matt predicted they'll maintain the rhetoric and structure it as responsible security checks rather than an outright reversal.

Jordan pushed back aggressively on the optimistic case. His argument was that as AI becomes exponentially more powerful, the political calculus within the Chinese government will shift decisively. It's one thing to champion open source when the models are impressive but fundamentally manageable. It's another when an open-weight model could theoretically be downloaded by a criminal syndicate, a separatist group, or a hostile nation state, and used to devastating effect. The Zhongnanhai instinct — the security-first reflex of China's leadership — will ultimately override commercial interests and diplomatic narratives. Once someone writes a memo about a specific incident where an open-weight model was used to cause real harm within China, the entire debate will transform overnight.

Kevin offered a sharp counterargument. He pointed out that this framing conflates model capability with whether open versus closed release actually matters. If a model is powerful enough to cause the kind of damage Jordan describes, then the real regulatory target should be compute infrastructure, not model weights. These frontier models are enormous — running them meaningfully requires thousands of GPUs and significant cloud computing resources. There are only a finite number of organisations worldwide with that level of compute. Regulating access to that infrastructure is far more practical and effective than trying to control the spread of model weights. Kevin argued that open models are, by definition, at least as safe as closed ones, because the research community can examine them for vulnerabilities and build defences — something you can't do with closed proprietary models.

Matt took a middle position. He acknowledged Kevin's points about compute requirements but wasn't willing to dismiss the risks entirely. Well-resourced non-state actors — criminal organisations, North Korea, other state-aligned groups — might have the technical capability and financial resources to deploy open-weight models for malicious purposes. He noted that OpenAI has recently reported detecting and blocking attempts by Chinese actors to use their models for generating harmful content. That kind of monitoring is simply impossible with open-weight models once they're released. However, Matt also pointed to emerging consensus among cybersecurity experts that we may be going through a transition period — a stretch of global cyber chaos — followed by a future state where software vulnerabilities are systematically eliminated and systems become provably secure. If China sees that path clearly, they might be willing to endure the transition period and then continue benefiting from open source.

The conversation's final major theme was US-China cooperation. What can these two countries actually agree on when it comes to AI? Matt framed the shared interest narrowly but precisely. Both countries should operate on the assumption that the other's intelligence agencies will use frontier models offensively. The NSA will target China; the MSS will target the United States. That's a given, and it's not going to change through dialogue. The genuine shared interest lies in controlling non-state actors — preventing criminal organisations, terrorist groups, and other malicious actors from accessing and deploying these capabilities. Even there, the incentives diverge around open source: Chinese companies are heavily invested in open-weight releases, while major American AI companies largely are not.

The most promising area for practical cooperation, Matt suggested, lies in domestic testing methodologies. Chinese frontier AI companies currently don't test for extreme risks — catastrophic cyber capabilities, biological weapon design, autonomous replication — at anywhere near the level of their American counterparts. There's no Chinese equivalent to the American AISI, the AI Safety Institute, with deep expertise in evaluating frontier model risks. Sharing testing knowledge, in a way that helps both sides identify dangerous capabilities without enhancing them, could be genuinely valuable. We're not talking about a treaty or shared regulatory thresholds — just practical knowledge sharing about how to sniff out dangerous capabilities before they're deployed.

Kevin advocated for a broader conception of bilateral dialogue. Beyond the existential risk scenarios, there are enormous everyday societal impacts where both countries face similar challenges. Labour displacement is perhaps the most significant. What happens when AI-driven productivity gains lead to大规模 layoffs? What's the legal framework? Both governments are wrestling with this right now, and companies in both countries have remarkably similar motivations. There's enormous value in simply sharing approaches — even if the solutions end up looking very different because of different political cultures.

This led to a revealing comparison of how the two systems approach AI-driven labour disruption. Matt pointed out that China's instinctive response is to prevent companies from firing workers who've been replaced by AI — essentially mandating that companies find alternative roles for displaced workers. The United States, by contrast, gravitates immediately toward clean economic solutions: equity stakes in companies, taxation, and redistribution through systems like UBI. Both approaches reflect deeply embedded political philosophies, and neither is clearly superior. Whether people derive more meaning from make-work jobs in a Chinese-style system or from government payments in an American-style efficiency model is a question that neither country has answered convincingly.

The conversation closed with a discussion of growing government entanglement with private AI companies — what Jordan wryly called state capitalism with American characteristics. The trigger was Sam Altman's reported offer of five percent of OpenAI's equity to the US government. Kevin expressed deep concern about this trajectory. He argued that the Fable saga — where GPT-5.6 was pre-released only to government-approved customers — looked uncomfortably like the kind of state-directed technology control more associated with Beijing. America seems to be copying China's least effective playbooks while ignoring its most useful ones. The prospect of every frontier lab offering equity stakes to the government, creating what Kevin called a bizarre new chapter in American state capitalism, raises fundamental questions about who benefits. If the government owns shares in AI companies, does that value flow to citizens, or does it disappear into Treasury? Kevin argued that it only works for the American people if those shares are distributed across the population — divided by 350 million and deposited into every citizen's account.

The conversation ended on a sobering note from Jordan. Today likely represents peak lab influence on AI policy. Right now, the most sophisticated policy thinkers work for the companies building the technology. The companies are writing the policy proposals, and they have more access to policymakers than anyone else. But this window is closing. As AI becomes a mass political issue — something voters actually cast ballots on — corporate influence will inevitably wane. The labs know this, which is why their policy timelines are all set before the 2028 election cycle. The next presidential campaign may well feature politicians running on populist platforms against the AI industry, promising to crack down in ways that make today's laissez-faire approach look quaint. The window for industry-shaped AI policy is closing, and what comes after remains the most consequential unknown in the AI governance landscape.
