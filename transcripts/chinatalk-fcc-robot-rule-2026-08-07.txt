The FCC's Quiet War on Chinese Tech — and Why Robots Are Next

A ChinaTalk interview with Adam Chan, national security counsel and senior advisor to FCC Chairman Brendan Carr. Chan directs the agency's new Council on National Security. This is a summary read of the conversation, originally published on ChinaTalk by Jordan Schneider on August 6, 2026.

---

When you think of the FCC, you probably think of spectrum licenses, broadcast media, maybe that little logo on the back of your phone. What you probably don't think is: one of Washington's most aggressive national security agencies. But that's exactly what the FCC has become.

The FCC has spent the past year quietly rewiring the American import market. First it was routers. Then drones. Then undersea cables. And as of last week — robots. All powered by a 1934 statute and two overwhelmingly bipartisan bills from 2020 and 2021, which turned what many thought was a sleepy spectrum regulator into a frontline weapon in the tech war with China.

To understand how we got here, you have to go back to the beginning. The Communications Act of 1934 — the law that created the FCC — states in its very first section that one of its two core purposes is national defense. That mandate has been there from day one. And over the last decade, it's been activated in a big way.

For Chairman Brendan Carr, national security has been a priority for years, and he's brought that focus to the chairmanship for the first time. He brought on Adam Chan as a unique position within the chairman's office to focus on and coordinate the national security side of things. They set up a new Council on National Security within the FCC to coordinate the national security work the Commission is doing across multiple bureaus and offices.

The key mechanism is something called the Covered List. Established under the Secure and Trusted Communications Networks Act of 2020 and the Secure Equipment Act of 2021 — both passed with overwhelming bipartisan support, with only a handful of no votes in the House — the Covered List lets the FCC block new models of equipment from being authorized for import or sale in the United States.

Here's how it works. The FCC can't put things on the Covered List by itself. Another national security agency or interagency body has to make the determination first. That becomes the legal predicate. Then the FCC updates the list and enforces the rules. This is different from how OFAC sanctions lists work, or the BIS Entity List, or the Pentagon's 1260H list of military companies. The FCC requires another agency to make the call first.

Once a device is on the Covered List, it can't get FCC authorization for import or sale. And here's the thing — virtually every electronic device needs FCC authorization, because virtually every electronic device emits radio frequency. That little FCC logo on your gadgets? That's the leverage point. It gives the FCC the power to shut off entire categories of products from entering the country.

The most recent action, taken just last week, targets advanced robotic devices — mobile robots generally — and power inverters. This is interesting because robotics is a pretty nascent industry. The FCC is getting ahead of where things might be five or ten years down the line, rather than waiting until the dependency is entrenched.

The motivation traces back to the Huawei and ZTE problem. A few years ago, the US realized its telecom networks were almost entirely reliant on Chinese equipment. It required a massive, expensive rip-and-replace effort to swap untrusted vendors for trusted ones. The thinking now is: let's avoid that situation in robotics, drones, and other industries of the future, before the dependency becomes entrenched and the cost of switching becomes prohibitive.

There are two main national security risks cited for the robot rule. First, remote access and cybersecurity — the ability for a foreign actor to remotely manipulate, surveil, or compromise a device. If you've seen videos of robots behaving badly online, you can imagine the concerns. There was even a recent story where someone playing with a DJI drone managed to gain access to a whole suite of robot vacuums. The data collection implications are significant — these devices collect a ton of data from inside people's homes.

Second, supply chain dependence. The US has a nascent robotics industry but is overwhelmingly reliant on foreign imports. If these devices become critical to manufacturing, military applications, or other industrial sectors, that dependency becomes a strategic vulnerability. And the best time to address a supply chain dependency is right now — it's only going to get harder as these industries grow and commercial incentives entrench the dependency further.

Now, you might wonder about the scope. Robot vacuums got swept in alongside humanoids, quadrupeds, and industrial robots. That seems aggressive. But there's a logic to it. The FCC prefers broad, simple definitions over narrow, complex ones. By broadly scoping in mobile robots generally, they avoid the whack-a-mole problem — where you ban one specific type of robot and companies just slightly redesign their product to dodge the rule. The blunt instrument approach has advantages: it allows for fast action, avoids endless carve-outs and loopholes, and sends a very strong market signal that gets industry's attention.

There's a weight threshold — the rule applies to robots over a certain size, which currently excludes the smallest toy-type devices. But the definition can evolve. On drones, for example, they initially had a broad definition, then later added an exemption for small toy drones that can't fly very far. Similar adjustments could happen with robots as the market develops.

There's also a conditional approval process built in. The Department of War can review applications from robotics companies saying, my robot is produced abroad but doesn't pose national security risks, and I'm planning to bring production to the United States. Companies that want to keep selling can apply — there are no fees, and any company from any country can apply. The process is fast — conditional approvals for drones have been coming out in just a few weeks, which is much faster than most national security reviews in the interagency process.

And there are built-in exceptions. Imports for use by the US government are unaffected. Imports in small quantities for product development or research are still allowed. So if you're a US robotics company looking to train an AI model on a foreign robot, or a defense contractor looking to practice counter-drone systems on foreign hardware, you can still do that. The FCC even clarified that counter-drone companies can import foreign drones to practice on — as long as it's in small quantities and complies with other regulations.

The industrial policy dimension is central to all of this. Chan makes the case that industrial policy IS national security policy — they're not separate buckets. If the US wants to be secure as a national security matter, it needs to be able to produce critical technologies domestically and not be entirely dependent on foreign countries, often highly concentrated in just one or two countries, for all of its capacity.

And the early results are encouraging. On drones alone, the FCC has seen over five billion dollars in investment since the restrictions, over a million square feet of new manufacturing capacity, and thousands of jobs created. On routers, there were zero companies producing routers in America before the action — now dozens have committed to expanding US production, with some breaking ground on new factories later this year. The lesson seems to be that when the market signal is clear, industry adapts.

The rule includes an onshoring requirement. To get conditional approval, companies must submit an onshoring plan describing how they'll move production to the US. There's a phased transition period — nobody expects 100% domestic production overnight. The executive branch recognizes the realism here. But they're not interested in granting broad exemptions to companies that just want to keep producing abroad indefinitely.

There's also a domestic content threshold. If a product meets the definition of a domestic end product — basically assembled in the US with 65% of components by value produced domestically — that's sufficient. The remaining 35% leaves room for components that can still be imported, including from allied countries. On the drone side specifically, there's also an exemption for any drone cleared on the Department of War's Blue UAS list — drones cleared for military sale.

Speaking of allies — the rules are country-neutral on their face. There's nothing explicitly targeting China. If a Chinese company like Unitree wanted to set up a factory in the United States and produce robots domestically, nothing in the FCC action would stop them. There are other tools — like CFIUS — for handling inbound investment concerns. The Chinese Ministry of Commerce has complained that the measures fly the banner of non-discrimination but in substance discriminate against Chinese enterprises and constitute protectionism. Chan's response is straightforward: the rules apply to everyone, there's nothing targeting any specific country, and any company from any country can apply for conditional approval at no cost.

One of the most interesting philosophical points in the interview is about how AGI-pilled you need to be to believe in the robot rule. Chan's answer is pragmatic. The rule makes sense regardless of your AI timeline. Maybe humanoid robots are a total dud and nobody gets one after 2027 — in which case, the rule doesn't matter much one way or the other. Maybe robotics takes off and these devices become critical infrastructure — in which case, this rule is all the more important. And even without AGI, warehouse robots, public safety quadrupeds like robot dogs, and industrial automation are already real markets with real national security implications. You don't need science fiction AI to justify concerns about being dependent on potentially dangerous foreign-sourced mobile robots.

There's a real tension here that Jordan Schneider presses on. Ukraine has had as much incentive as any nation could possibly have to create a drone industry isolated from Chinese parts, and they haven't been able to do it. There are serious questions about whether the US can actually build a competitive domestic ecosystem, given Chinese dominance in rare earths, magnets, and critical components. But Chan's view is that markets tend to clear. Oil prices haven't spiked to five hundred dollars a barrel despite the Strait of Hormuz situation. Startups are building out capacity for brushless motors, batteries, and magnets at scale. Two or three years from now, the US parts industry for drones and robots will look very different.

The FCC's philosophy here is worth highlighting. Simple but dumb beats big-brained and nuanced. A blunt instrument that acts fast and sends a clear signal often outperforms a sophisticated rule that takes years to implement, years more to amend, and confuses everyone in the meantime. Especially when you're dealing with fast-evolving industries where today's cutting-edge product is obsolete in five years. The Covered List works best precisely because it's a fast-evolving industry — the robot of today is not really going to be on the market in five years anyway.

It's also worth noting the political economy. You hear a lot from the Boston Dynamics of the world — established US robotics companies who welcome the removal of Chinese competition — and from VC-backed startups trying to build domestic alternatives. You hear less from the long-tail consumer who might face higher prices or fewer choices. That's a standard pattern in trade policy — concentrated benefits, dispersed costs.

Looking ahead, the FCC shows no signs of slowing down. There are open dockets on submarine cable security, equipment testing lab integrity — what they call the bad labs proceeding — and further updates to the Covered List expected. The enforcement side is also heating up. The FCC recently caught a company that apparently lied on its application form about producing in the United States, where the manufacturer listed in the application confirmed they'd never even heard of the company. They're investigating drone companies that may be white-labeling covered equipment to try to get around restrictions.

The FCC may be small — about 1,200 employees and a 400 million dollar budget, tiny compared to the Defense Department, Justice Department, Commerce, or Treasury — but its statutory tools are broad, its expertise is deep with a high concentration of lawyers and engineers, and its national security work has been unanimously bipartisan across all votes. All the national security rules and enforcement actions have been bipartisan and unanimous, including with Democratic commissioners. That means this work is likely to endure beyond the current administration, which makes it all the more impactful.

There's a running joke in Washington about the FCBA Chairman's Dinner — apparently it's like telecom prom, where all the telecom lawyers gather for a charity dinner and the Chairman gives a funny speech. It's a reminder that beneath the national security headlines, the FCC is still a small agency full of nerds who like learning and care about getting the technical details right.

The broader lesson from this interview is that the most consequential policy actions in the US-China tech competition may not come from the agencies you expect. Not the Pentagon, not the Commerce Department, not the Treasury — but the FCC, using a 1934 law and a 2020 covered list mechanism to systematically choke off Chinese tech imports in industry after industry, from telecom equipment to routers to drones to undersea cables to robots. Each action individually seems narrow and technical. Together, they add up to a de facto industrial policy — one that says: if you want to sell the future of technology in America, you'd better build it in America.
