China's Quantum Flywheel: Six Months of Party Mobilization.

A deep dive by Elias X. Huber, author of the China Meets Quantum blog, originally published in ChinaTalk on August 12, 2026. Summary read, approximately fifteen minutes.

The Chinese quantum sector evolved rapidly over the first six months of twenty twenty-six. The many Western think tank reports written on China's quantum ecosystem in twenty twenty-five already feel extremely dated. ChinaTalk is a reader-supported publication, and today's deep dive by Elias Huber, a researcher at Fraunhofer Singapore and Singapore's Centre for Quantum Technologies, catches you up on how much changed in just half a year.

The structure of Beijing's mandates for China's quantum sector was clarified by the fifteenth Five-Year Plan in March twenty twenty-six, where quantum technologies are listed first among the future industries. This was reinforced by Xi Jinping's Future Industry speech to the Politburo, his essay in Qiushi, the Party's official theoretical journal, and countless articles and study sessions in response from government ministers, local cadres, and quantum start-ups alike.

It truly feels like a new industry is being willed into existence. Like a flywheel slowly picking up speed. Today's deep dive covers three main areas: the scale of raw commercial expansion with skyrocketing investments through over forty funding rounds, a neutral atom quantum computing boom approaching thirty hardware companies, and a multitude of new quantum funds. Second, the implementation of policy tools and platforms, including jiebang guashuai challenge-based bidding, scenario cultivation, and concept-verification and pilot-testing centers. And third, how China's domestic patient capital has evolved and stepped up since US outbound investment controls.

Let's start with the quantum mandate and its scaffolding. The CCP is betting big on innovation and tech-driven development, with quantum technologies singled out as a future industry of strategic importance for both China's development and its security. Reading the high-level mandates, they contain interesting tensions. There's the push for self-reliance and breaking through chokepoints, while simultaneously promoting international collaboration and going global. Another contradiction is the call for unified national planning alongside demands for differentiated local development and avoiding blindly following the trend. Or take the role of basic research: be bold, exploratory, and original, but also target bottleneck technologies.

To translate these abstract mandates into concrete action, China uses several mechanisms. The first is scenario cultivation, the principle that industry sets the questions and science provides the answers. This marks a shift from a science-push model to an industry-pull model. Hefei launched a Thousand Scenarios action plan, where the local government acts as matchmaker between quantum tech companies and industry players to serve real industrial needs. Zhejiang Province issued a similar plan targeting quantum cryptography applications in government, finance, healthcare, and power sectors, along with product industrialization of quantum gravimeters, magnetometers, and atomic clocks. Matchmaking conferences were held in Suzhou to align supply and demand, with the first session focused on cryogenics, a second on chip design and manufacturing, and a third on quantum software and applications.

The second mechanism is jiebang guashuai, which literally means exposing the list and assuming control. This is a challenge-based open bidding system where industry players and the government post public challenges and chokepoints, and multiple teams compete to solve them with outcome-based funding carrots. China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology issued seventeen quantum challenges in early twenty twenty-five, with clearly benchmarked tasks for control electronics, dilution refrigerators, and quantum error correction. Local governments have issued similar challenge lists. The system has also been used in hackathon-style competitions run by CETC's quantum arm, with students from junior college to PhD submitting solutions over months, and prize money of one hundred thousand RMB for the overall champion.

The chokepoint framing, literally stuck neck in Chinese, is often invoked in the context of Western export restrictions that have been in place since the early twenty-twenties and escalated in twenty twenty-four. Breakthroughs are celebrated in this language: domestic silicon production of the purity required for silicon-based quantum computing, a portable rubidium clock claimed to be the world's first, and domestic dilution refrigerators claimed to surpass international competitors.

China is also building out physical infrastructure to bridge the gap between lab and factory. The Hefei National Quantum Lab, one of the first of a new batch of national laboratories, manages China's flagship quantum program and has established a national platform with facilities in Shanghai, Beijing, Jinan, and Guangdong. Concept-verification centers focus on the first mile of technology transfer from research into application. The Zhongguancun Quantum Technology Incubator in Beijing tracks seventy-nine quantum research teams and has provided services to twenty-four companies. In Hunan, a concept-verification center specifically focused on quantum navigation was established in April.

Pilot-testing platforms handle the last mile, helping transition prototypes into full production. In Wuxi, Turing Quantum and the Shanghai Jiao Tong Wuxi Photonic Chip Research Institute operate China's first photonic quantum chip pilot line. Construction began in twenty twenty-two, and the six hundred and fifty million RMB production line began operation in twenty twenty-five, reportedly shortening R and D cycles from ten months to just two weeks. Origin Quantum recently raised three billion RMB in pre-IPO funds, earmarked for ten-thousand-qubit superconducting quantum chip pilot lines.

Now, let's turn to the enterprises themselves. For a while, online analysis belittled the role of the private sector in China's quantum ambitions. That view is increasingly short-sighted. The Party explicitly calls for clustering innovation resources toward enterprises, cultivating tech-leading enterprises, supporting state-owned enterprises as both the national team and main force, and nurturing large numbers of small and medium tech companies, specialized enterprises, single-product champions, and unicorns.

These enterprise designations are not just labels. They imply different access to support policies such as direct subsidies, tax credits, access to credit and grants, facility usage, and procurement opportunities.

In the first half of twenty twenty-six, Photon Box Research Institute reports seventy-six global quantum financing deals, of which the majority, forty-four, were in China. In aggregate dollar terms, China still trails the US, with at least one point five billion dollars invested in China compared to nearly two billion in the US. But Chinese quantum enterprise financing increased thirty-fold from the first half of twenty twenty-five, when only eight deals were recorded. This surpassed funding in the rest of the world, excluding the US, by about a factor of three.

However, Chinese analysts offer a more nuanced view. They argue the current funding boom should be compared to the twenty twenty-two to twenty twenty-three period in the US, when quantum VC averaged around three hundred million dollars per quarter, prior to the IPOs and consolidation that followed. The current Chinese boom may be a temporal lag rather than a sprint to leadership.

The funding flows through two tracks. First, established incumbents, often with full-stack ambitions and over five years since establishment, make up the largest rounds and are preparing IPOs. Companies like Origin Quantum, Boson Quantum, and SpinQ are exploring public listings. Second, the largest share of rounds by number goes to very young startups founded since twenty twenty-two, with many appearing in twenty twenty-five and even twenty twenty-six. At least four quantum companies had their first funding round in just the month of July twenty twenty-six. This goes counter to the consolidation trend seen in the Western commercial quantum sector.

By the author's count, there are now at least twenty-eight quantum computing hardware startups in China: nine in superconducting, nine in neutral atoms, four in ion traps, and six in photonics. Only one company, Origin Quantum, is active in semiconductor-based quantum computing.

The neutral atom boom is one of the big stories of twenty twenty-five and twenty twenty-six. This is true globally, but especially so for China. In Shanghai alone, four new neutral atoms quantum computing companies have been founded since twenty twenty-five: Taiyi Quantum, Atom Core Photonics, Zhongqi Wuliang, and Buchou Quantum. A fifth supply chain player, Xuanxiang Technology, recently announced atomic optical tweezers enabling future platforms with more than a million controlled atoms.

Another significant development is the emergence of quantum software and algorithm startups. Six quantum computing software companies, all but one founded since twenty twenty-five, secured investments in the first half of twenty twenty-six. This matters because a lack of commercial ecosystem maturity has been responsible for Chinese entities lagging Western counterparts in quantum computing cloud platforms and application environments.

The investment activity is regionally clustered. Beijing leads with thirteen funded companies, followed by Shanghai with eight, Zhejiang with six, and Hefei with six. Distinctive regional specialization is appearing. Beijing has ventured into quantum software and algorithms, while Shanghai has established itself as a hub for neutral atoms and photonic quantum computing. In the big picture, Hefei, Beijing, and Shanghai remain the national R and D and talent backbone, while coastal regions in Shanghai, Zhejiang, and the Greater Bay Area are rapidly moving into commercial applications.

Where is the money coming from? The largest rounds are dominated by state capital, including state-owned enterprises and state-linked financial institutions. Local government guidance funds have been especially active in Beijing and Shanghai, including cross-regional investments. The National VC Guidance Fund, with a massive one hundred billion RMB fiscal anchor, a twenty-year horizon, and a one trillion RMB mobilization target, made its first direct quantum investment in April twenty twenty-six through its Yangtze River Delta sub-fund, investing in Turing Quantum. Industry giants from CATL to Ant and Geely actively co-led rounds. And venture capital and private equity was very active, though primarily in early-stage rounds, with the caveat that even VC investments often include government limited partners.

New dedicated quantum industry funds are popping up across provinces. In January, Anhui announced a two hundred million RMB fund focused on early-stage quantum communication and computing. In April, Sichuan launched a one billion RMB quantum fund targeting photonic chips, superconducting quantum computing, and quantum communication. In May, Hubei registered a one hundred million RMB fund in Wuhan. Also in May, Beijing launched a one hundred million RMB fund targeting the optical quantum computing supply chain. And in June, China Telecom established a massive one point five billion RMB quantum industry venture fund focused on quantum communication, computing, and precision measurement.

Interestingly, established quantum companies are participating in fund management. QuantumCTek contributes to the China Telecom fund management, and QBoson contributes to the Beijing Jinggong Quantum fund. This aligns with the Chinese industrial policy concept that chain leader enterprises should coordinate and build up their respective supply chains, mentoring new entrants and guiding capital into bottlenecks and dependencies.

iFlytek's establishment of a Quantum-AI integration venture is particularly noteworthy. They explicitly adopted a no-KPI approach, with the chairman stating that iFlytek's role is defined as patient capital, providing funding, AI technology, and industry expertise, but not interfering with the direction of scientific research. This venture was established jointly with a Tsinghua researcher whose team recently trapped eleven thousand neutral atoms. This contrasts sharply with the previous quantum labs of Baidu, Alibaba, and JD, whose focus on near-term payoffs was incompatible with the immaturity of quantum computing.

The government is also providing services that go well beyond what Western governments typically do. In April, Anhui launched a Quantum Industry Public Service Package providing patent databases, IP protection services, trademark strategies, and a Quantum Industry Patent Navigation Report, largely free of charge. These fill a need addressed by consultancies and specialized law firms in the West, a market which in China is underdeveloped due to trust deficits, walled gardens, and a general unwillingness to pay for professional data services.

On the financial services side, a quantum tech insurance consortium has been established to provide customized insurance solutions. The nation's first insurance policy for environmental anomaly losses of quantum equipment was recently signed in Guangzhou. The full pipeline includes grants combined with investment, invest-first-take-equity-later models, procurement support for first products, equity investments, and exit opportunities through mergers and acquisitions and public listings.

So where does this leave us? China's most prominent public quantum figure, Pan Jianwei, described the current situation as an investment carnival, calling out speculation and commercial promises not backed by actual technology. He suggested the state establish a professional third-party quantum technology evaluation center. Some domestic coverage has been healthily de-hyping the sector.

But in the big picture, the overall direction and tools thoughtfully address every dependency needed to turn this future industry into a real industry within the coming decade. If the timing is broadly right, and the primary challenges of quantum technologies are engineering, supply chain scaling, and application scenarios, then this may well work. It wouldn't be the first time. Think electric vehicles, batteries, or photovoltaics.

The Chinese quantum sector is increasingly decoupled from the West due to export controls. Companies have to make do with domestic suppliers who are rapidly stepping up. Announcements frequently highlight the large fraction of domestic components. New entrants appear enabled by breakthroughs from domestic suppliers, like the four new Shanghai-based neutral atoms startups that went barely noticed on the English internet.

At the same time, the sector remains global in important ways. All four of the new Shanghai neutral atoms companies have overseas-linked co-founders. Chinese analysis of Western quantum research is extensive and often excellent. And the Party explicitly calls for participating in global governance, striving for discourse power in international standards, and promoting co-building of standards, co-consultation on rules, and co-promotion of industries.

From a Western perspective, simply shutting all doors to the Chinese quantum sector is not a smart option. Interesting and world-leading work is bound to come out of this industrialization effort. Collaboration in foundational research, coordination on standards, and dialogue to govern quantum computers that can break encryption, increasingly plausible within the next five years, are promising areas for mutually beneficial cooperation. Engagement is valuable, if for no other reason, to help Western players anticipate competition in the global market.

At the same time, export controls and research security measures have expedited a path that cannot simply be walked back. The Chinese push to eliminate foreign chokepoints will not reverse even if controls were lifted today. Chinese talent that already left Western institutions will not simply return overseas.

On export controls and research security, compromises are hard. But there are individual steps where a reasonable middle ground is viable. Clarifying conditions and forums under which engagement is politically safe. Delineating the truly security-relevant areas in the vast field of quantum science. Improving incentives for Western researchers to work in Chinese labs and companies, to reduce concerning asymmetries in the flow of talent and knowledge.

China's quantum flywheel is starting to crank. Navigating the resulting landscape of competition, security, and engagement requires political choices. Not to take notice isn't a choice. It's negligence.
